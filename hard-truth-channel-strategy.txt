PART 4 — SCENE PACKS
================================================================================

THE ECONOMICS PROBLEM, AND THE ONLY ANSWER TO IT

An 18-minute episode needs 60–90 visual beats. Generating those bespoke every
week is unaffordable and it is why most faceless long-form channels degrade
into a stock-footage slurry by episode ten.

So you do not build episodes. You build a BANK once, and you recombine it.
PACK 1 is ~20 clips that appear in every episode forever. PACK 2 is the 10–12
clips unique to one episode. The ratio you are aiming for in a finished cut is
roughly 70% bank, 30% episode-specific. At two episodes a week that is about
ten new generations a week, which is sustainable.

HOUSE STYLE — CARRIED OVER FROM THE KLARNA AND CREDIT CARD PACKS, UNCHANGED
  Stylised low-poly 3D. Matte clay shading, near-photorealistic surface texture
  on paper, card, fabric and wood. All human figures have COMPLETELY BLANK
  featureless heads — no eyes, nose or mouth, smooth matte tan block.
  Palette: deep navy #1E3A5F · terracotta #E85F42 · cream #F5F0E6 ·
  near-black #0A0C10. Cool neutral grade, one warm amber accent per frame.
  Single hard key, one long real shadow, deep falloff. Slow camera or none.
  There is no fast movement anywhere in this house style.

WHAT CHANGES FOR LONG-FORM
  16:9 HORIZONTAL, 1920x1080. Subject sits in the left or right third with the
  opposing two-thirds open and dark — that open space is where burned-in
  numbers and document scans land. This is the inverse of your Shorts framing
  and it is the most common thing to get wrong on the first shoot.
  Generate at 10s where the model allows and trim; bank clips get reused at
  many different lengths, so longer masters are worth the cost once.

NEGATIVE PROMPT — paste once, applies to every clip in both packs
  facial features, eyes, mouth, nose, lips, eyebrows, face, portrait, text,
  lettering, numbers, logos, brand names, watermark, signature, cartoon
  outlines, cel shading, flat vector, anime, 2D illustration, distorted hands,
  extra fingers, extra limbs, morphing objects, warping architecture, camera
  shake, whip pans, zoom bursts, blurry, low resolution, oversaturated, HDR
  glow, plastic sheen, uncanny, stock-photo smiles, clutter, busy background

[ ACT 1 — THE ARTEFACT ]

[serious] Every year a letter arrives telling you what you will pay to carry on
having something you already have.

You have had it for years. Nothing about it has changed. You have not moved
house, you have not crashed the car, you have not made a claim.

And the number has gone up.

Most people have an explanation ready for this. Inflation. Costs. The economy.
Everything is going up.

That explanation is comfortable and it is usually wrong. Because if you had
phoned the same company that same afternoon, and asked for the same product as
a stranger, you would very often have been quoted less than the letter in your
hand.

Same company. Same product. Same person. Two prices.

[pause]

The difference between those two numbers is not a cost. It is not risk, it is
not admin, it is not inflation.

It is a charge for staying.

[serious] And for a long time, in this country, nobody had a name for it that
you were ever going to hear.

  — TITLE CARD lands here, on K19. Not before. —

[ ACT 2 — THE NAME ]

The industry had a name for it. Of course it did. You do not build a system
this precise without vocabulary.

They called it price walking. Sometimes dual pricing. The regulator, when it
finally arrived, used a blunter phrase — the loyalty penalty.

Here is how it works, and it is worth being exact, because the vagueness is
where it survives.

A company sells you something on a contract that renews. Insurance. Broadband.
Energy. A phone. A gym.

To get you in the door, they quote you a price that is deliberately low.
Sometimes it is below what serving you actually costs them. That is not a
mistake. That is acquisition, and it is a budget line.

Then the contract renews. And this time the price is not set by what it costs
to serve you. It is set by a different question entirely.

The question is: how likely is this person to leave?

[serious] Read that again, because everything in this film sits on it. The
renewal price is not a measure of your risk. It is a measure of your inertia.

If you are the kind of customer who checks — who compares, who phones, who
threatens to cancel — the system learns that, and it holds your price down. Not
as a reward. As a defence.

And if you are the kind of customer who does not check. Who is busy. Who has a
newborn, or two jobs, or a parent who is ill, or simply too much going on to
audit a direct debit — then the system learns that too.

And it walks your price up. A little. Every year. Never enough in one step to
make you act.

[pause]

That is the craft of it. The increase is calibrated to sit just underneath the
threshold at which you would do something about it.

[serious] Your attention has a price. And they know it more precisely than you
do.

Let me put numbers on it, because the vagueness is doing a lot of work here and
numbers take it away.

Say a policy starts at four hundred and twenty pounds. Say the walk is eight
per cent a year — modest, well inside what this practice was found to do.

Year one you pay four hundred and fifty three. Year two, four hundred and
eighty nine. Year three, five hundred and twenty nine. Year four, five hundred
and seventy one. Year five, six hundred and seventeen.

Meanwhile the price a stranger is quoted for the identical product has not
moved. It is still four hundred and twenty.

[pause]

Across those five renewals you have paid five hundred and sixty one pounds more
than the person who phoned up for the first time.

But look at the step sizes, because this is the actual engineering. The biggest
single jump in that whole sequence is forty five pounds. Spread across a year,
that is under four pounds a month.

[serious] Nobody cancels over four pounds a month. That is the design
specification. Every individual increase is calibrated to be smaller than the
effort of leaving, and the sum of them is not.


There is a version of this you have probably already told yourself — that this
is just how markets work, that the shopper gets the deal and the lazy pay more,
and that on some level that is fair.

Hold that thought. Because a regulator looked at exactly that argument, took it
seriously, and rejected it.

[ ACT 3 — THE BAN ]

In 2021, the Financial Conduct Authority — the body that regulates financial
services in the United Kingdom — finished a long piece of work on how home and
motor insurance was actually priced.

What it found was not a market that happened to produce this outcome. It was a
market where this outcome was the strategy. Systematic. Modelled. Deliberate.

So it did something regulators do not often do. It did not ask for clearer
disclosure. It did not require a warning on the letter. It did not tell firms
to be fairer in general terms.

It banned the practice outright.

[serious] From the first of January, 2022, an insurer offering you a renewal on
your home or motor policy has to offer it at a price no higher than it would
quote you, that day, as a brand new customer of the same risk profile buying
the same product.

They gave it a name in the rules — the equivalent new business price. And the
principle underneath it is very simple, and much larger than insurance.

Your loyalty may not be priced.

[pause]

They closed the side doors too, because they had watched this happen before.
Auto-renewal was tightened. Cash incentives to bring new customers in cannot
be dangled at strangers unless they are offered to the people who already
stayed.

And the FCA published what it expected the ban to be worth to the people who
had been paying it.

Four point two billion pounds. Over ten years.

[pause]

Sit with that number, because it is the answer to the question you did not
quite ask earlier.

That is not a market inefficiency. Four point two billion pounds is not
friction, or admin, or the ordinary cost of people being busy.

That is the measured value of a mechanism. That is what your inattention was
worth, in one country, in two product lines, to one industry.

[serious] And a regulator looked at it, and did not call it competition. It
called it a penalty.

[ ACT 4 — THE MOVE ]

So that is the good news, and it is real. If you insure your home or your car
in the UK, this specific thing is not being done to you anymore.

Now here is the hard truth this film is actually about.

[pause]

The practice did not stop. It moved.

Because of how the ban was written. Read it carefully and you find it is not a
ban on charging people for staying. It is a ban on charging people for staying
IN HOME AND MOTOR INSURANCE.

The rule is bounded by the FCA's jurisdiction, and the FCA regulates financial
services. It does not regulate your broadband provider. It does not regulate
your mobile network, or your gym, or your streaming subscriptions, or the
software you renew every year without looking.

The mechanism was never specific to insurance. It only needs three things, and
they are everywhere.

One. A contract that renews by default.
Two. A price the seller can change at renewal.
Three. A customer who probably will not check.

[serious] Wherever those three conditions sit together, the mechanism is
available. And it is sitting in most of the direct debits leaving your account
this month.

The introductory rate that quietly ends. The contract that rolls onto a
standard tariff nobody would ever have chosen deliberately. The membership that
is easy to start on a phone and requires something considerably more effortful
to stop. The annual renewal that bills on a date you have long since forgotten
you agreed to.

None of that is fraud. Every one of those is disclosed somewhere. You signed.

And that is exactly the point.

Because look at what the two options actually cost you in effort.

Staying costs nothing. It requires no decision, no phone call, no password you
have forgotten, no thirty minutes on hold, no form. Staying happens by itself
while you are asleep. It is the default, and defaults are free.

Leaving costs an afternoon. Find the account. Find the renewal date. Find what
a stranger pays. Phone the number that is harder to find than the sign-up
button was. Wait. Explain yourself to someone whose job is to keep you. Be
offered a slightly better price to stay, which is itself the admission that
the price was never fixed.

[pause]

That gap — free to stay, expensive to leave — is not an accident of how
companies happen to be organised. It is the product. Every hour of friction
between you and the exit is an hour of margin, and it is designed with the
same care as anything else they sell you.

[serious] They are not charging you for the service. They are charging you for
the door being heavy.


[pause]

The defence of this practice has always been that the information is available.
That anyone can compare, that switching is free, that the diligent customer is
rewarded and the rest have only themselves to blame.

But the FCA had that argument in front of it, backed by an industry with far
better lawyers than you have, and it still concluded that a system which
prices human limitation is not a system that has earned its money.

[serious] Because that is what is actually being sold here. Not a service.
Not a product. The predictable gap between what a person intends to do and
what a tired person actually gets round to doing.

[ ACT 5 — THE LESSON AND THE FIX ]

There is a reading of this film that stops at "check your renewals", and that
reading is not wrong. It is just small.

So do the small thing first, because it works.

Once a year, take an hour. Get the list of everything that renews — your bank
statement will give it to you faster than your memory will. For each one, find
what a new customer pays today. Not what you paid. What a stranger is quoted.

Where there is a gap, phone and ask for the new-customer price. You will get it
more often than you expect, because keeping you is cheaper than replacing you,
and their model already knows that.

And cancel the thing you have not used since the spring.

That hour is worth more per hour than almost anything else you will do this
year. But it is not the lesson.

[pause]

The lesson is this.

The loyalty penalty was not made illegal because it was wrong. Nothing about it
became wrong in January 2022 that was not wrong in December 2021.

It was made illegal in one place, because in that one place, somebody had the
authority and finally used it.

Everywhere else, the same mechanism, doing the same thing to the same people,
is simply carrying on. Not because anyone decided it was acceptable there.
Because nobody with the power to stop it has looked yet.

[serious] That is worth understanding properly, because it changes what you
think you are looking at. When something extractive is legal, you have learned
almost nothing about whether it is right. You have only learned where the
jurisdiction ends.

[pause]

And there is one more thing, underneath all of it.

Look at what is actually being charged for. Not a good. Not a service. Not
labour, not risk, not anything anyone made.

Time. And your failure to spend enough of it watching.

[calm] A real sale takes its profit once. At the moment of exchange. Both sides
know the number, both sides agree, and then it is finished — it does not grow
afterwards while you sleep.

This does. It compounds quietly on the assumption that you are tired. It is
priced in your distraction and collected in your absence.

[serious] Which means the defence is not vigilance. You cannot out-attend a
system that is designed around the certainty that your attention runs out.

The defence is fewer doors. Fewer things that renew. Fewer automatic
relationships with companies that are modelling how long you will not notice.

[warmly] Be harder to price.

Every one of these has a mechanism, and every mechanism has a name once you go
looking. Subscribe — I go through one of them properly, every week.
