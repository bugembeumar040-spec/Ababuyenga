card" is: yes, and not here. The ujrah card is a real, licensed, ordinary
product in the Gulf and Malaysia. It has not reached the UK retail market. That

thirds is the version you assemble yourself tonight.

audience arriving here already watched that. If you find yourself writing a line
that argues rather than instructs, cut it.

doing the pacing.

[serious] There is a halal credit card. You just can't get one in Britain.
In the Gulf it's an ujrah card. The bank charges one flat fee.
Same plastic. Same limit. No interest — because time isn't in the price.
Licensed. Regulated. Ordinary. It just hasn't crossed the Channel.
[warmly] So build the version you can have tonight.
One. A separate account. Funded before you spend, not after.
Two. The ceiling is yours. You set it, in an app, once.
Three. A fee you choose — never a rate that chooses you.
[serious] And be honest about what you lose. Section seventy-five. On credit, the

ACCURACY — the four claims that need to survive a comment section
================================================================================

"UJRAH CARD" — real and current. Ujrah (fee/wage) structured cards are issued in
Malaysia and across the GCC: the issuer's revenue is a disclosed flat service fee
rather than interest on an outstanding balance. Some issuers layer tawarruq or
qard-hasan structures underneath, and those variants are contested among
scholars. The VO says "the bank charges one flat fee", which describes the ujrah
model specifically and does not endorse every product marketed as Islamic. Keep
that phrasing.

"YOU CAN'T GET ONE IN BRITAIN" — accurate as of writing for mainstream retail.
UK Islamic banking is well established for home finance, current accounts and
savings, but a Sharia-compliant consumer credit card is not a normal high-street
product here. Do not name a specific bank on screen as not offering one — product
lines change and a dated screenshot is a correction you will have to pin.

"SECTION 75 · £100–£30,000" — Consumer Credit Act 1974. Where a purchase is made
using credit, the lender is jointly and severally liable with the seller for
breach of contract or misrepresentation. The commonly cited range is that the
cash price of the item must be over £100 and no more than £30,000. Two nuances
worth having ready for the comments: it can apply even when only part of the
price was paid on the card, and debit-card protection is chargeback, which is a
card-scheme rule rather than a statutory right. The caption says "on credit the
bank shares the liability", which is right and avoids overstating the scope.

"KEEP ONE CARD, CLEAR IT SAME DAY" — this is the line that will draw the most
argument, because holding a conventional card means being party to an
interest-bearing contract even if no interest is ever paid, and scholars differ
on that. The script does not issue a ruling — it presents a trade-off and states
its cost. That is the same posture as both previous packs. If you want the
cautious cut, replace scene 10's VO with "Or drop credit entirely, and accept the
gap" and change CAP 1 to "OR DROP IT ENTIRELY", which costs you 0.4s and no
retention. Pick one before you record; do not hedge both ways in the VO.
