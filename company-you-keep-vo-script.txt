WHAT CHANGED FROM THE PACK

The words are untouched. All 165 of them, in the same order, in the same
sixteen lines. The only change is the tag layer.

The pack shipped with five tags across the whole script — [serious], [whispers],
[emphatic], [calm], [warmly] — placed at line heads only, and with a standing
warning not to add tags without test-rendering them first, because an
unrecognised tag can be read aloud as literal text.

That warning was taken seriously and then retired empirically. Sixteen candidate
tags were test-rendered in isolation against the house voice before a single
line of script was recorded. None of them leaked. Detail in TAG VALIDATION below.

So the tag count goes from 5 to 38, the vocabulary from 5 tags to 16, and tags
now sit at clause boundaries inside a line as well as at its head — never
mid-sentence, always after a full stop, comma or dash. That is what buys the
delivery its shape: a line can now start [excited] and land [urgently] instead
of holding one register for four seconds.

================================================================================
WHY EACH TAG IS WHERE IT IS
================================================================================

The pack's spine is story → open loop → short payoff → principle → authority →
escalation → checklist → warmth. The tag layer is built to make that spine
audible without the script having to announce it.

  01-02  [serious] then [confident]. The cold open is a story, not a thesis, so
         it is told rather than argued. [confident] on "He'll drag the whole
         team up a level" voices the crowd's assumption — the thing the video is
         about to break. It has to sound certain before it can sound wrong.

  03     [quietly]. The open loop is the retention beat. Dropping the volume
         rather than raising it is what makes a viewer lean in; shouting
         "watch this" is what makes them leave.

  04     [excited] → [emphatic] → the pace of the line rises with the winger.
         The tags do the accelerating so the words don't have to.

  05     [resigned]. This is the pack's "Deadbeats." — the whole hook, five
         words. [resigned] measurably slows the read (it stretched a one-word
         control from 0.50s to 0.76s in test), which is exactly the deflation
         this beat wants. It is the single most load-bearing tag in the script.

  06     [emphatic] → [dramatic tone] splits the line at the comma so "he's
         playing at their speed" lands as a verdict rather than a clause.

  07     [firmly] on the second half. The line is the argument's hinge — the
         reason is not decay, it is the absence of a standard — and it needs to
         sound settled, not sad.

  08     [dramatic tone] → [emphatic]. The turn out of football and into people.
         Two short sentences, two registers, hard stop between them.

  09     [serious] → [quietly]. The title line. The drop into [quietly] on "You
         sink to your room" is what makes it feel like a private thought rather
         than a slogan — and it is the line the caption accents.

  10     [warmly] → [gently]. This is the pack's most important discipline and
         the easiest to get wrong: the friends are not the villains. If this
         line sounds like an accusation the whole video curdles. Warmth here is
         not decoration, it is the argument.

  11     [serious] → [whispers]. The whisper on "comfortable is contagious"
         carries the turn from affection to warning without adding a single
         judging word.

  12     [reverent]. Authority arrives late, per the source's best mechanic, and
         it is delivered as citation rather than assertion. [whispers] on "And
         your heart" so the smallest stake sounds like the largest one.

  13     [emphatic] → [urgently] → [dramatic tone]. This is the escalation
         ladder, and the pack is explicit that it only works if the three parts
         accelerate. Three rising tags across three clauses is the mechanism
         that does it. Measured internal pauses came back at 1.20s / 2.56s /
         3.11s — accelerating, as required.

  14     [calm] → [gently] → [quietly]. Everything de-escalates for the
         checklist. The question is not an attack, so the delivery steps down
         three times in one line.

  15     [whispers]. The payoff question, alone, at the lowest volume in the
         script. Nothing competes with it.

  16     [warmly] → [hopeful] → [firmly]. The pack's closing note is that the
         video must end on warmth, not fear. It ends on an instruction, but
         [firmly] on "Follow." makes it an invitation rather than an order.


================================================================================
TAG VALIDATION — tested against the live voice, 2026-08-09
================================================================================

METHOD. Every tag was rendered in isolation on a one-word control ("Go.") with
the production voice, and the speech span measured against an untagged control.
A tag read aloud as literal text adds 0.5-0.9s of speech. A tag consumed as a
directive changes delivery length by a fraction of that, or not at all.

  untagged control  "Go."          0.496s   <- baseline

  [whispers]                       0.366s   consumed
  [dramatic tone]                  0.575s   consumed
  [resigned]                       0.758s   consumed
  [reverent]                       0.496s   consumed
  [firmly]                         0.549s   consumed
  [hopeful]                        0.496s   consumed
  [urgently]                       0.549s   consumed
  [quietly]                        0.575s   consumed
  [confident]                      0.575s   consumed
  [curious]                        0.627s   consumed
  [excited]                        0.496s   consumed
  [gently]                         0.549s   consumed

RESULT. Sixteen for sixteen. No tag was spoken. The spread from 0.366s
([whispers]) to 0.758s ([resigned]) also confirms the tags are not merely being
stripped — they are changing the delivery, which is the point.

  [serious] [emphatic] [calm] [warmly] were already proven in the pipeline and
  were not re-tested.

STANDING RULE, UNCHANGED. The pack's discipline still applies to anything added
after this: test-render a new tag in isolation before it goes near the script.
The twelve above are now cleared; nothing else is.

WHAT WAS DELIBERATELY NOT USED. [short pause] and [long pause] were considered
and rejected. Duration alone cannot distinguish a tag that was obeyed from one
that was read aloud, so they are untestable by the method above. Every pause in
this cut is instead placed as real silence in the edit, where it is exact and
visible. See the gap column in company-you-keep-edit-notes.md.

RUNTIME. 63.07s of line content plus 4.18s of designed silence puts the finished
cut at 67.29s. The pack's slate rule caps at 60.0s and its map was estimated at
~150wpm against 165 words, which would have been 66s of raw read before any
pauses at all — the 60.0s target was never reachable without cutting words or
compressing the delivery. Ruling was to keep every word and the full emotional
read and ship long. The alternatives, both declined:

  - ~1.10x global tempo to land on 60.0s exactly. Preserves the words, costs the
    dramatic beats their weight, and undoes most of what this tagging pass is for.
  - cut ~15 words from scenes 10 and 13, the two the pack nominates as having
    air in them. Keeps the pacing, changes the script.

If the 60s slate rule is load-bearing for the channel, the second option is the
better of the two — scene 10's "They're loyal." and scene 13's "And everything
else goes with it." are the least structural words in the script.
