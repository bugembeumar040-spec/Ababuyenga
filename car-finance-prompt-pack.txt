STYLE BIBLE — derived from the sheets and the rendered frames
================================================================================

RENDER      Flat 2D vector cartoon illustration. Thick, uniform dark brown
            outline on every shape, same weight throughout — no tapering, no
            sketchiness. Flat colour fills. Shading is one slightly darker tone
            of the same hue, in a simple soft shape (under a jaw, inside a
            sleeve, one side of a cylinder). No gradients on skin. No texture,
            no grain, no photorealism, no 3D.

GROUND      A single soft cream-shadow ellipse under a standing figure or
            object. Nothing else. No cast shadows with direction.

BACKGROUND  Warm cream #F7EFDD, very faint vertical gradient, otherwise empty.
            Environments are built from a handful of flat shapes only — the
            Deadbeat frame renders a whole room as one lamp (cone + line +
            small circle) and one table edge. Match that restraint.

PALETTE     Sample the exact values off the sheets; these are close.
            Cream ground      #F7EFDD      Outline / type    #3D2B23
            Terracotta accent #B0503A      Muted label grey  #8A7D72
            Skin, light       #F2C4A8      Skin, mid-tan     #C89468
            Skin, mid-brown   #D0A077      Institution grey  #7A7A7A
            Shoes / dark      #3A2E28      Panel white       #FBF7EF

FACES       Simple black dot eyes. Thin dark eyebrow strokes. One small mouth
            line. A short nose line or none. Expression is carried almost
            entirely by the eyebrows and the mouth line — see both expression
            sheets. Nothing rendered, nothing detailed.

THE RULE    People have faces. The institution does not. That contrast is the
            single strongest device in your visual language and it does the
            moral work of the film without a word of VO. Never put a face on
            the grey figure. Never take one off a named character.

  Three years of payments, and then you give it back. That is not the problem.

  On a PCP the finance company is the owner for the whole term. You are the
  registered keeper — the person the DVLA writes to — which is not the same
  thing and was never meant to be. So you are renting. Renting is renting, and
  a lease has a name in Islamic finance: ijara. Not owning the car is fine.

  Here is what isn't.

  A PCP splits the car into two parts. The part you pay off over the term, and
  a lump deferred to the very end — the optional final payment, the balloon,
  the Guaranteed Future Value. Most people never pay the balloon. Handing the
  car back is the normal exit; it is what the product is designed around.

  But the balloon sits inside the financed amount for the entire term, and
  interest is charged on the whole of it. Every month, for three years, part of
  your payment is a price for time on a slab of car you were always going to
  walk away from. That is why the monthly looks affordable and the total cost
  of credit doesn't.

  Two flows, not one. Money leaving you on a buyer's schedule. A logbook that
  never moves, on a tenant's terms. You carry the cost of ownership and hold
  the rights of someone renting.

  THE TWO-MINUTE CHECK:

  1. Find the optional final payment on your agreement. It is on page one, in
     its own box, and it is usually the largest single number on the document.
  2. Find the total amount payable and the total charge for credit. Subtract
     the cash price of the car. What is left is what time cost you.
  3. Decide honestly which one you are. If you intend to hand it back, you are
     renting — so compare it against a straight lease, where you are not
     financing a balloon you will never pay. If you intend to keep it, you are
     buying — so compare the total charge for credit against saving for it.

  Separately, and worth two minutes of its own: the FCA's motor finance redress
  scheme covers PCP and HP agreements taken out between 6 April 2007 and
  1 November 2024 where commission arrangements weren't properly disclosed. It
  opened on 30 June 2026 for agreements from 1 April 2014, and 31 August 2026
  for earlier ones. Around 12.1 million agreements are in scope. It is free to
  use and you do not need a claims company. Check the FCA's own site for where
  it currently stands — parts of it are subject to legal challenge.

  That scheme is about disclosure. This video is about the structure underneath
  it, which no redress scheme touches.

  This describes how these contracts are built. It is not a religious ruling —
  check with someone qualified before you act on it.

"THE LOGBOOK WAS NEVER YOURS" — correct on both PCP and HP. The finance company
retains title until the final payment (on a PCP, the optional final payment plus
any purchase fee). The customer is the registered keeper, and the V5C itself
states that is not proof of ownership. Do not say "you don't own the V5C" — the
distinction is keeper versus owner, and getting it precisely right is what makes
the rest of the video credible. Scene 02 stages exactly this and nothing more.

"INTEREST IS CHARGED ON THE BALLOON" — correct and central, so be exact about
why. The optional final payment is deferred, not excluded: it remains part of
the outstanding balance for the whole term, so interest accrues on it
throughout. That is precisely why a PCP monthly is lower than an HP monthly on
the same car while the total charge for credit is not proportionally lower. If a
commenter says "but I never paid the balloon" — that is the point, and scene 07
already shows it being carried away.

"IJARA" — a genuine lease contract in Islamic finance, and conceding it up front
is the honest move. But keep the concession narrow. The film says a lease is
legitimate; it does not say a PCP is an ijara. It isn't — an ijara's rental is a
price for use, and a PCP's finance charge is a price for time on deferred
principal. Sami is shown renting and content; he is never shown on a PCP. Do not
let a caption or a comment reply blur those two.

earlier ones; covers 6 April 2007 to 1 November 2024; roughly 12.1 million
